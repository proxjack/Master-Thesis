\section{Motivation and Objectives}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 3
\begin{frame}{Why energy harvesting?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Wireless sensor networks and IoT devices have grown very fast:
              \alert{millions of small nodes} need to be powered.
        \item Batteries are expensive to maintain, have a finite life, and in many
              embedded or rotating installations \alert{cannot be replaced at all}.
        \item They are also a poor choice for the environment.
    \end{itemize}
    \vfill
    \begin{block}{Energy harvesting}
        Convert energy already available in the environment into electrical power,
        so that the node powers itself for its whole lifetime.
    \end{block}
    \vfill
    This thesis targets \textbf{rotating motion}, converted through
    \textbf{Variable Reluctance Energy Harvesting (VREH)}.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 4
\begin{frame}{The variable reluctance principle}
    \begin{columns}[T]
        \column{0.46\textwidth}
            \small
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Magnet and coil stay \alert{completely still}.
                \item Only a toothed wheel rotates across the air gap.
                \item The passing teeth modulate the reluctance, hence the flux
                      linked by the coil: an EMF is induced.
            \end{itemize}
            \vspace{0.5ex}
            \textbf{Advantages}
            \begin{itemize}
                \item No moving electrical parts.
                \item Easily scaled to different shaft sizes.
                \item Very small counter torque.
            \end{itemize}
        \column{0.52\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{VR_theory.pdf}\\[1ex]
            {\scriptsize Stationary pickup unit (magnet, pole piece, coil)
             facing the rotating toothed wheel.}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 5
\begin{frame}{The six-phase MP-VREH}
    From single-unit prototypes for railway monitoring to an optimised
    \alert{six-phase} design for large vehicles (buses): six pickup units,
    electrically shifted by $60^\circ$, mounted around the shaft.
    \vfill
    \begin{table}
        \centering
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{cccccc}
            \toprule
            rpm & $f$ (Hz) & $V_\text{Lpk}$ (V) & $R_\text{coil}$ ($\Omega$) & $L_\text{coil}$ (mH) & $P_o$ (W)\\
            \midrule
            100 & 36.7  & 0.91 & 6 & 21.68 & 0.41\\
            200 & 73.3  & 1.35 & 6 & 21.46 & 0.91\\
            300 & 110.0 & 1.59 & 6 & 21.27 & 1.26\\
            400 & 146.7 & 1.74 & 6 & 21.12 & 1.51\\
            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
    \end{table}
    \vfill
    Target operating range: \alert{100--400 rpm}, i.e.\ 20--80 km/h for a typical
    large commercial vehicle. Each phase delivers less than $2\ \text{V}$ peak:
    the rectifier choice becomes critical.
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 6
\begin{frame}{Research gap and question}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item More phases mean more power, but a much harder electronics problem:
              \alert{six AC signals}, all shifted in time, all to be rectified.
        \item Previous work compared rectifier topologies for a
              \alert{single-phase} VREH only.
        \item How to condition the power of a six-phase harvester has never been
              studied systematically.
    \end{itemize}
    \vfill
    \begin{alertblock}{Research question}
        Which interface circuit extracts the most power from the six-phase
        MP-VREH, across its entire 100--400 rpm speed range?
    \end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 7
\begin{frame}{Objectives and approach}
    A complete path, from theory to a working prototype:
    \vfill
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Model} the harvester: electrical model of one coil,
              extended to the six-phase system.
        \item \textbf{Propose} rectifier topologies able to combine six phases.
        \item \textbf{Simulate} each candidate in MATLAB/Simulink, sweeping the
              compensation capacitor and the load.
        \item \textbf{Design and build} a single reconfigurable prototype board.
        \item \textbf{Measure} every topology on the same hardware, on the same
              test bench.
        \item \textbf{Assess} compatibility with commercial power management ICs.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section{Modelling and Proposed Topologies}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 8
\begin{frame}{Coil model and matching}
    \begin{columns}[T]
        \column{0.52\textwidth}
            \small
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Each coil: ideal source in series with $R_\text{Coil}$ and
                      $L_\text{Coil}$.
                \item At low speed a purely \alert{resistive} load is enough to
                      extract the maximum power.
                \item As speed (and frequency) grows, $\omega_e L_\text{Coil}$
                      can no longer be neglected: the matched-load condition is lost.
                \item A \alert{series compensation capacitor} $C_m$ cancels the
                      inductive reactance and restores it:
            \end{itemize}
            \begin{equation*}
                C_m = \frac{1}{\omega_e^{2}\,L_\text{Coil}}
            \end{equation*}
            valid at one frequency at a time.
        \column{0.46\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Coil_model.pdf}\\[2ex]
            \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Matching_condition.pdf}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 10
\begin{frame}{From waveform to simulation}
    \begin{columns}[T]
        \column{0.44\textwidth}
            \begin{itemize}
                \item The RMS voltage of each coil was computed as a function of
                      speed.
                \item Each source was then replaced by an \alert{equivalent
                      sinusoid}, scaled through the measured crest factor.
                \item It delivers the same power as the real waveform, so the
                      simulation stays realistic while remaining tractable.
            \end{itemize}
        \column{0.54\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Voltage_comp.pdf}\\[0.5ex]
            {\scriptsize FEM prediction, raw measured peak and crest-factor
             corrected value.}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 11
\begin{frame}{Strategy 1: FWR bridges}
    \begin{columns}[T]
        \column{0.52\textwidth}
            \small
            The six phases split into \alert{two three-phase groups}
            ($k=0,2,4$ and $k=1,3,5$), each feeding a standard three-phase bridge.
            \vspace{1ex}
            \begin{itemize}
                \item \textbf{Star (Y)}: line voltage is $\sqrt{3}$ times the
                      phase EMF, so the fixed two-diode drop costs relatively less.
                \item \textbf{Delta ($\Delta$)}: line voltage equals the coil EMF,
                      no amplitude gain, but no floating node.
            \end{itemize}
            \vspace{1ex}
            Expectation: \alert{higher open-circuit voltage for the star
            connection}. Both are carried forward as candidates.
        \column{0.46\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{FWR.pdf}\\[1ex]
            {\scriptsize Three-phase bridge for one group; the current path always
             crosses two diodes.}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 12
\begin{frame}{Strategy 2: NVC bridges}
    \begin{columns}[T]
        \column{0.52\textwidth}
            \footnotesize
            The six phases split into \alert{three two-phase groups}, each pairing
            phases $180^\circ$ apart ($k=0,3$; $1,4$; $2,5$) in \textbf{anti-series}:
            \begin{equation*}
                v_A - v_B = e_k - e_{k+3} = 2\,e_k
            \end{equation*}
            ideally double the voltage, at the cost of a doubled series impedance.
            \vspace{0.5ex}
            \begin{itemize}
                \item Four MOSFETs with \alert{cross-coupled gates}: two conduct at
                      a time, no gate driver needed.
                \item They bypass the parallel Schottky diodes, replacing the fixed
                      drop with a small $R_\text{on}$; below
                      $V_\text{off}\approx1.2\ \text{V}$ the bridge falls back to
                      diode behaviour.
            \end{itemize}
        \column{0.46\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{NVC.pdf}\\[1ex]
            {\scriptsize Negative voltage converter for one anti-series pair.}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Simulation results}
    \begin{columns}[c]
        \column{0.33\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Y_sweep/Y_Power_sweep.pdf}\\
            {\scriptsize Star}
        \column{0.33\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{D_sweep/D_Power_sweep.pdf}\\
            {\scriptsize Delta}
        \column{0.33\textwidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{NVC_sweep/NVC_Power_sweep.pdf}\\
            {\scriptsize NVC}
    \end{columns}
    \vfill
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Output power across the speed range, for several fixed values of $C_m$.
        \item At $100\ \text{rpm}$, the \alert{worst case} that sets the lower bound
              of what the system can deliver, the NVC gives $69.0\ \text{mW}$:
              about $6\times$ the delta connection ($10.7\ \text{mW}$) and nearly
              double the star one ($36.7\ \text{mW}$).
        \item The NVC is also the most efficient topology at every speed.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 17
\begin{frame}{Measured results}
    \begin{table}
        \centering
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{lccc}
            \toprule
            & FWR (Star) & FWR (Delta) & NVC\\
            \midrule
            $C_m$ ($\mu\text{F}$) & 630 & 630 & 430\\
            $R_L$ ($\Omega$)      & 7   & 5   & 6\\
            $P_O$ at $100\ \text{rpm}$ (mW) & 47.6 & 14.9 & \alert{57.7}\\
            $P_O$ at $400\ \text{rpm}$ (mW) & \alert{433.3} & 310.7 & 425.7\\
            $\eta_\text{Rec}$ at $100\ \text{rpm}$ & 0.410 & 0.308 & \alert{0.568}\\
            $\eta_\text{Rec}$ at $400\ \text{rpm}$ & 0.564 & 0.488 & \alert{0.701}\\
            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
    \end{table}
    \vfill
    \begin{itemize}
        \item At low speed the NVC still wins, as simulation predicted.
        \item Above roughly $250\ \text{rpm}$ the \alert{star connection overtakes
              it in raw output power}, something simulation did not predict.
        \item The NVC keeps the best efficiency at every speed (70\% against 56\%
              at top speed).
        \item A bus does not run at a single speed: the best topology depends on
              the vehicle speed profile.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 18
\begin{frame}{Simulation vs measurement}
    \begin{table}
        \centering
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{lccc}
            \toprule
            $C_m$ at the optimum ($\mu\text{F}$) & Star & Delta & NVC\\
            \midrule
            Simulated & 490 & 455 & 260\\
            Measured  & 630 & 630 & 430\\
            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
    \end{table}
    \vfill
    The measured optimum is consistently \alert{higher} than the simulated one,
    for all three topologies. Two causes, both pushing in the same direction:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The real coil inductance is somewhat \alert{lower} than the nominal
              value, and $C_m \propto 1/L_\text{Coil}$.
        \item The ceramic compensation capacitors \alert{lose part of their
              capacitance} under the real DC bias.
    \end{itemize}
    A further gap on efficiency, growing with speed, is consistent with
    frequency-dependent losses that the fixed-resistance coil model does not
    represent.
\end{frame}

\section{Contributions and Conclusions}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 20
\begin{frame}{Original contributions}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The \alert{first systematic study} of power conditioning for a
              six-phase VREH; previous work compared rectifiers for a single phase.
        \item Two ways of combining six phases into working rectifier circuits,
              adapted and validated for this harvester.
        \item A single \alert{reconfigurable platform}: every topology compared on
              the same hardware, under the same mechanical conditions, a fair and
              controlled comparison that is rare in this field.
        \item A current-sensing methodology with individual calibration of every
              sensor, trustworthy down to the milliwatt.
        \item The key finding: \alert{there is no single best topology}. The NVC
              wins on efficiency and at low speed, the star connection on raw power
              at high speed, a nuance that only appears on real hardware.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 21
\begin{frame}{Conclusions and future work}
    \small
    \begin{block}{Conclusions}
        The NVC and the star connection are the two strongest candidates, each
        suited to a different part of the speed range; the delta connection is
        consistently the weakest. NVC and delta connect safely to any PMIC tested,
        while the star connection needs protection above $250$--$300\ \text{rpm}$.
    \end{block}
    \begin{alertblock}{Limitations}
        The coil model does not capture losses that grow with frequency; the
        3D-printed housing is sensitive to vibration, which limits how repeatable
        the measurements can be at high speed.
    \end{alertblock}
    \begin{block}{Future work}
        An adaptive compensation network tracking the optimal $C_m$ as speed
        changes, and a full hardware test of the harvester connected to a real
        PMIC, to measure the true end-to-end efficiency.
    \end{block}
\end{frame}

\chapter{Theoretical Background}

This chapter introduces the physical and circuit-level concepts required to motivate the design choices made in the following chapters.
In this chapter, starting from Faraday's law, an electrical
model of the variable-reluctance energy harvester (VREH) is
derived. The model is then used to discuss the maximum
power transfer theorem and the role of impedance matching

\section{Electrical Model of the Variable-Reluctance Harvester}


\subsection{Faraday's law}
\label{sec:faraday_law}

The Faraday's law is the starting point for any electromagnetic transducer, which relates the electromotive force $\varepsilon(t)$ (EMF) induced in a closed loop wire to the time rate of change of the magnetic flux $\Phi(t)$ linked to the loop. 

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(t) = -\frac{\dd \Phi_i(t)}{\dd t}
    \label{eq:faraday}
\end{equation}

For a coil made by $N_t$ turns sharing the same flux, the total linked flux is $\Lambda(t)=N\,\Phi_i(t)$ and the induced EMF becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(t) = -\frac{\dd \Lambda(t)}{\dd t} = -N_t\frac{\dd \Phi_i(t)}{\dd t}.
    \label{eq:faraday_N}
\end{equation}

In case of a coil consisting of $N_t$ turns mechanically spaced one respect to each other leading to different linked flux, the induce voltage can be described by

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(t) = -\frac{d}{dt} \cdot \sum_{n=i}^{N_t} \Phi_i(t)
\label{eq:faraday_dis}
\end{equation}


where $\Phi_i$ is the flux linkage associated to a single turn of the coil\cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}.

\subsection{Variable Reluctance Principle}

Unlike conventional electromagnetic transducers, in which the EMF is induced by a relative motion between a permanent magnet (PM) and a pickup coil, a variable-reluctance energy harvester (VREH) keeps both the magnet and the coil stationary. The magnetic flux linked to the coil is instead modulated by the passage of a rotating, toothed ferromagnetic wheel in front of a stationary pickup unit: as a tooth or a slot alternately faces the pickup unit, the reluctance of the magnetic circuit changes periodically, and it is this reluctance variation -- rather than any bulk motion of the magnet -- that drives the flux change $\Phi_i(t)$ in \eqref{eq:faraday_dis} \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}. Because the rotating part carries no magnet, no coil and no electrical connection, the resulting transducer is mechanically simple, robust, and easily rescaled to shafts of different diameters \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

    \label{fig:VREH_basic_struct}
\end{figure}


The pole-piece and the toothed wheel are both made of soft ferromagnetic material, while the PM is a hard ferromagnetic material providing a magnetomotive force (MMF) $\mathcal{F}_M$ that can be taken as constant. As the wheel rotates, the pickup unit cycles between two extreme relative positions: the \emph{aligned} position, where a pole of the pickup unit faces a tooth of the wheel and the air-gap reluctance is minimum, and the \emph{unaligned} position, where the pole faces a slot and the reluctance is maximum \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018,xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}. The corresponding flux swings between a maximum $\Phi_{max}$ and a minimum $\Phi_{min}$, so that the relevant design quantity is the flux difference $\Delta\Phi = \Phi_{max}-\Phi_{min}$ between the two extremes. Fig.~\ref{fig:Airgap_swing} illustrates this swing directly: the air-gap flux density is sharply peaked at the aligned position and nearly collapses at the unaligned position, confirming that the flux change is concentrated within a narrow angular window around each tooth-slot transition.

\subsubsection*{From flux swing to output power}

For a wheel with $N_t$ teeth rotating at angular velocity $\omega$, the electrical frequency of the induced voltage is
\begin{equation}
    f_c \;=\; \omega\, N_t.
    \label{eq:freq_teeth}
\end{equation}
At low rotational speeds the coil impedance is dominated by its resistance $R_{Coil}$, so that maximum power is transferred to a purely resistive load matched to it, $R_L = R_{Coil}$; under this condition the load voltage is related to the open-circuit coil voltage $V_c(t)$ of \eqref{eq:faraday_dis} by
\begin{equation}
    V_L(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\,V_c(t).
    \label{eq:vload}
\end{equation}
Assuming the time-varying flux to be an ideal sinusoidal wave, uniformly distributed in the $N_c$ turns of the coil, the peak-to-peak voltage developed across the matched load follows from \eqref{eq:faraday_dis} and \eqref{eq:vload} as \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}
\begin{equation}
    V_{L,pp} \;=\; -\frac{1}{2}\,N_c\,\frac{\Phi_{max}-\Phi_{min}}{\frac{1}{2}T_c} \;=\; -N_c\, f_c\, \Delta\Phi,
    \label{eq:vpp}
\end{equation}
where $\frac{1}{2}T_c$ is the time taken by the wheel to rotate from the aligned to the unaligned position, i.e.\ from a tooth to a slot centred on the pole-piece. 


Because $V_{L,pp}$ in \eqref{eq:vpp} is the peak-to-peak amplitude of an (assumed) sinusoidal load voltage, its root-mean-square value is $V_{L,rms} = V_{L,pp}/(2\sqrt{2})$, so that the RMS power dissipated in the matched load is \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;=\; \frac{V_{L,rms}^2}{R_L} \;=\; \frac{V_{L,pp}^2}{8\,R_{Coil}} \;=\; \frac{(\omega\, N_t\, N_c)^2}{8\,R_{Coil}}\,(\Delta\Phi)^2,
    \label{eq:power_law}
\end{equation}



where the last equality follows from substituting \eqref{eq:freq_teeth} into \eqref{eq:vpp}. Equation \eqref{eq:power_law} shows that the harvested power scales quadratically with the rotational speed $\omega$, the flux swing $\Delta\Phi$, and the number of coil turns $N_c$, and linearly with the number of teeth $N_t$, while it is inversely proportional to the coil resistance $R_{Coil}$.

For a given application (i.e.\ fixed $\omega$ and $N_t$), the design of the pickup unit therefore splits into two largely independent optimization problems \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    P_L &\;\propto\; \frac{N_c^2}{R_{Coil}}, \label{eq:subfunc_coil}\\
    P_L &\;\propto\; (\Delta\Phi)^2 \;=\; \left(\frac{\mathcal{F}_M}{\Delta\mathcal{R}}\right)^2, \label{eq:subfunc_flux}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\Delta\mathcal{R} = \mathcal{R}_u - \mathcal{R}_a$ is the difference between the total loop reluctance at the unaligned and aligned positions. Sub-function \eqref{eq:subfunc_coil} is a winding-design problem: for a coil of resistivity $\rho_{wire}$ occupying a cross-section of height $h_{Coil}$ and width $W_{Coil}$ inside the pole-piece, it can be shown that \eqref{eq:subfunc_coil} reduces to \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;\propto\; \frac{h_{Coil}\,W_{Coil}\,f_w}{\rho_{wire}},
    \label{eq:coil_appendixA}
\end{equation}
with $f_w<1$ the winding fill factor of the coil; for a fixed available cross-section, output power is thus maximized simply by maximizing the copper fill of the winding window. Sub-function \eqref{eq:subfunc_flux}, in contrast, is a magnetic-circuit design problem: with the MMF $\mathcal{F}_M = H_c\,h_M$ fixed by the magnet coercivity $H_c$ and its height $h_M$, maximizing $\Delta\Phi$ reduces to maximizing the reluctance swing $\Delta\mathcal{R}$ between the two extreme rotor positions.

\subsubsection*{Magnetic-circuit model of the reluctance swing}

The reluctance of a homogeneous flux-path element of length $l$, cross-section $A$ and relative permeability $\mu_r$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R} \;=\; \frac{l}{\mu_0\,\mu_r\,A}.
    \label{eq:reluctance_def}
\end{equation}
Since the soft ferromagnetic pole-piece and toothed wheel have a relative permeability several orders of magnitude larger than that of air ($\mu_r\approx 1$) or of the PM ($\mu_r\approx 1.05$), their contribution to the loop reluctance is negligible, and $\Delta\mathcal{R}$ is dominated by the air gap and by the magnet's own reluctance $\mathcal{R}_M = h_M/(\mu_0\mu_M A_M)$ \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}. Denoting by $\mathcal{R}_{g,a}$ and $\mathcal{R}_{g,u}$ the air-gap reluctance at the aligned and unaligned positions, respectively, the flux at each extreme follows from Hopkinson's law as $\Phi_{max}=\mathcal{F}_M/(\mathcal{R}_{g,a}+\mathcal{R}_M)$ and $\Phi_{min}=\mathcal{F}_M/(\mathcal{R}_{g,u}+\mathcal{R}_M)$, so that the flux swing of \eqref{eq:subfunc_flux} can be written explicitly as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\Phi \;=\; \mathcal{F}_M\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{g,a}+\mathcal{R}_M} - \frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{g,u}+\mathcal{R}_M}\right).
    \label{eq:flux_extremes}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:flux_extremes} makes explicit that, once the magnet material and the air-gap length are fixed by mechanical constraints, $\Delta\Phi$ is governed by the pickup-unit geometry surrounding the air gap, namely the tooth height $h_{Tooth}$ (which sets $\mathcal{R}_{g,u}$, since a taller tooth increases the reluctance seen at the unaligned position up to a saturating knee at approximately $h_{Tooth}\approx 3\ \text{mm}$) and the magnet height $h_M$ (which enters both $\mathcal{F}_M$ and $\mathcal{R}_M$, so that an optimal, non-trivial value of $h_M$ exists) \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

For a fixed available volume, expressed as a hollow cylinder of overall diameter $d_o$, shaft diameter $d_{Shaft}$ and depth $l_o$, the volumetric power density of the harvester is
\begin{equation}
    p_v \;=\; \frac{P_L}{V_{VREH}}, \qquad V_{VREH} \;=\; \frac{\pi}{4}\,l_o\,(d_o-d_{Shaft})^2,
    \label{eq:power_density}
\end{equation}
and, once the pole pitch and slot width are fixed, the available radial space $C = h_{Coil}+h_{Tooth}$ must be split between the coil and the tooth. Since $P_L$ increases monotonically with $h_{Coil}$ through \eqref{eq:coil_appendixA} but only up to the knee point through $h_{Tooth}$ in \eqref{eq:flux_extremes}, for a given $C$ there is an optimal $h_{Coil}/h_{Tooth}$ ratio that maximizes $p_v$ \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}. Similarly, setting $\dv{\Delta\Phi}{h_M}=0$ in \eqref{eq:flux_extremes} yields a closed-form optimal magnet height,
\begin{equation}
    h_M^{\ast} \;=\; \mu_M\,\frac{k_2}{k_1}\,\frac{W_{g,a}}{W_{g,u}}\,\sqrt{l_{g,a}\,l_{g,u}},
    \label{eq:optimal_hm}
\end{equation}
where $l_{g,a}$, $l_{g,u}$, $W_{g,a}$, $W_{g,u}$ are the effective air-gap length and width at the aligned and unaligned positions, and $k_1,k_2>1$ are correction factors accounting for flux leakage and for the MMF drop in the finite-permeability soft iron, respectively \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

\subsection{Electrical Thevenin Model of the Pickup Unit}
\label{sec:Electrical_model}
% The electrical model and its consequent derivation is important to study the system and t o of a single pick up unit 

% Il modello elettrico e la sua conseguante modellazione sono estremamente importanti e permettono di studiare il sistema dal punto di vista elettrico\cite{xu2022rotational}.



The Faraday law derivation of Section~\ref{sec:faraday_law} gives the ideal electromotive force induced in the coil, but it does not yet describe how that voltage appears at the physical terminals of the pickup unit. A real coil is made of a finite length of copper wire, so it has a nonzero winding resistance, and it carries a current that produces its own magnetic flux, so it also has a nonzero self inductance. Both effects sit in series with the ideal source, since the same wire that generates the electromotive force also carries the resistance and the flux linkage that define $R_{Coil}$ and $L_{Coil}$.

\subsubsection*{Circuit equation}

Let $e(t)$ denote the ideal electromotive force predicted by \eqref{eq:faraday_dis}, and let $i(t)$ be the current flowing out of the coil into the external circuit. Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law around the loop formed by the source, the winding resistance, the winding inductance and the external circuit gives
\begin{equation}
    e(t) \;=\; R_{Coil}\, i(t) \;+\; L_{Coil}\,\frac{\dd i(t)}{\dd t} \;+\; v(t),
    \label{eq:kvl_coil}
\end{equation}
where $v(t)$ is the voltage measured at the coil terminals, available to the power conditioning circuit. Equation \eqref{eq:kvl_coil} is the defining relation of the series generator model adopted throughout this thesis, an ideal voltage source $e(t)$ in series with a resistance $R_{Coil}$ and an inductance $L_{Coil}$, feeding the external circuit at the coil terminals.

\subsubsection*{Winding resistance}

The winding resistance follows directly from the resistivity of the wire, its total length and its cross sectional area,
\begin{equation}
    R_{Coil} \;=\; \rho_{wire}\,\frac{N_c\, l_t}{A_w},
    \label{eq:rcoil_def}
\end{equation}
where $N_c$ is the number of turns, $l_t$ is the mean length of a single turn, and $A_w$ is the cross sectional area of the wire. If the coil occupies a winding window of height $h_{Coil}$ and width $W_{Coil}$ with a copper fill factor $f_w$, the wire cross section can be written as $A_w = f_w\, h_{Coil}\, W_{Coil} / N_c$, so that \eqref{eq:rcoil_def} becomes
\begin{equation}
    R_{Coil} \;=\; \rho_{wire}\,\frac{N_c^2\, l_t}{f_w\, h_{Coil}\, W_{Coil}}.
    \label{eq:rcoil_fillfactor}
\end{equation}
Treating $l_t$ as approximately constant for a fixed pole piece geometry, \eqref{eq:rcoil_fillfactor} reproduces the proportionality $N_c^2 / R_{Coil} \propto f_w\, h_{Coil}\, W_{Coil} / \rho_{wire}$ already used in \eqref{eq:coil_appendixA} to optimize the winding cross section \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

\subsubsection*{Self inductance and its dependence on rotor position}

The self inductance of a coil linked to a magnetic circuit of total reluctance $\mathcal{R}$ is, by the same reluctance formalism used for Hopkinson's law in \eqref{eq:reluctance_def},
\begin{equation}
    L_{Coil} \;=\; \frac{N_c^2}{\mathcal{R}}.
    \label{eq:lcoil_def}
\end{equation}
Because the pickup unit is a variable reluctance device, the reluctance seen by the coil's own flux changes with the rotor angle in exactly the same way as the reluctance that shapes the induced electromotive force. The self inductance therefore swings between two extremes,
\begin{equation}
    L_{max} \;=\; \frac{N_c^2}{\mathcal{R}_{g,a}+\mathcal{R}_M}, \qquad
    L_{min} \;=\; \frac{N_c^2}{\mathcal{R}_{g,u}+\mathcal{R}_M},
    \label{eq:lcoil_extremes}
\end{equation}
reached at the aligned and unaligned positions respectively, mirroring the flux extremes already given in \eqref{eq:flux_extremes}. For circuit level analysis, such as the impedance matching study of Section~\ref{sec:impedance_matching}, this position dependent inductance is normally replaced by a single lumped value $L_{Coil}$, obtained either as an average over one electrical period or as a direct measurement with an impedance analyzer at the frequency of interest. This is the value referred to as $L_{Coil}\approx210\ \text{mH}$ later in this chapter.

\subsubsection*{Phasor representation}

For a sinusoidal steady state at the electrical frequency $f_c$ of \eqref{eq:freq_teeth}, the time domain relation \eqref{eq:kvl_coil} can be written in phasor form as
\begin{equation}
    E \;=\; I\left(R_{Coil} + j\,\omega_e\,L_{Coil}\right) + V,
    \label{eq:phasor_source}
\end{equation}
where $E$, $I$ and $V$ are the phasors associated with $e(t)$, $i(t)$ and $v(t)$, and $\omega_e = 2\pi f_c$. Equation \eqref{eq:phasor_source} shows that, seen from its electrical terminals, the pickup unit behaves as an ideal source $E$ in series with the impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z_s \;=\; R_{Coil} + j\,\omega_e\,L_{Coil},
    \label{eq:source_impedance_def}
\end{equation}
which is precisely the Th\'{e}venin source impedance used in \eqref{eq:source_impedance} for the impedance matching analysis of Section~\ref{sec:impedance_matching}. The series generator, resistance, inductance model derived in this section therefore provides the electrical foundation on which the maximum power transfer condition of the next section, and the power conditioning topologies compared in Chapter~\ref{ch:topologies}, are built.


\subsection{Maximum Power Transfer and Impedance Matching}
\label{sec:impedance_matching}

The previous section modelled the pickup unit as a Th\'{e}venin source, an open circuit EMF $V_c(t)$ in series with the coil's own impedance. At low electrical frequency this impedance is essentially the winding resistance $R_{Coil}$, and matching a resistive load to it, $R_L = R_{Coil}$, is enough to extract the maximum available power, as already used in \eqref{eq:power_law}. As the rotational speed increases, however, the coil inductance $L_{Coil}$ can no longer be neglected, and the condition for maximum power transfer must be generalized. This section derives that general condition and explains why it becomes a central design constraint for a harvester that must operate between 100 and 400\,rpm, the target range of this thesis.

\subsection{Th\'{e}venin model of the source and the load}

Let the coil be represented, at the electrical frequency of interest, by its Th\'{e}venin equivalent, an RMS open circuit phasor voltage $V_c$ in series with the source impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z_{s} \;=\; R_{Coil} \;+\; j\,\omega_e\,L_{Coil},
    \label{eq:source_impedance}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_e = 2\pi f_c$ is the electrical angular frequency and $f_c$ is the electrical frequency defined in \eqref{eq:freq_teeth}. The load presented to the coil by the power conditioning circuit is likewise represented, at the same frequency, by a generic complex impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z_{L} \;=\; R_{L} \;+\; j\,X_{L}.
    \label{eq:load_impedance}
\end{equation}
Here $R_L$ is the resistive part of the load and $X_L$ its reactive part, which can be inductive ($X_L>0$) or capacitive ($X_L<0$) depending on how the load is realized.

\subsection{Power delivered to the load}

With $Z_s$ and $Z_L$ in series, the RMS current flowing in the loop is
\begin{equation}
    I \;=\; \frac{V_c}{Z_s + Z_L},
    \label{eq:loop_current}
\end{equation}
and the average power dissipated in the resistive part of the load is
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;=\; |I|^2 R_L \;=\; \frac{|V_c|^2\,R_L}{(R_{Coil}+R_L)^2 + (\omega_e L_{Coil}+X_L)^2}.
    \label{eq:power_general}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:power_general} is the generalization of \eqref{eq:power_law} to a complex load. Setting $L_{Coil}=0$ and $X_L=0$ recovers the purely resistive case already used earlier in this chapter.

\subsection{The complex conjugate matching condition}

Equation \eqref{eq:power_general} can be maximized in two independent steps, since $R_L$ and $X_L$ appear in separate terms of the denominator.

First, for any fixed $R_L$, the denominator is minimized, and $P_L$ is therefore maximized, by cancelling the reactive term. This requires
\begin{equation}
    X_L \;=\; -\,\omega_e L_{Coil}.
    \label{eq:reactance_cancel}
\end{equation}
With this choice, \eqref{eq:power_general} reduces to
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;=\; \frac{|V_c|^2\,R_L}{(R_{Coil}+R_L)^2}.
    \label{eq:power_resistive_stage}
\end{equation}
Second, differentiating \eqref{eq:power_resistive_stage} with respect to $R_L$ and setting the result to zero,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial P_L}{\partial R_L} \;=\; |V_c|^2\,\frac{R_{Coil}-R_L}{(R_{Coil}+R_L)^3} \;=\; 0
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    R_L \;=\; R_{Coil}.
    \label{eq:resistive_match}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:reactance_cancel} and \eqref{eq:resistive_match} gives the well known maximum power transfer theorem in its general, complex form,
\begin{equation}
    Z_L \;=\; Z_s^{*} \;=\; R_{Coil} \;-\; j\,\omega_e L_{Coil}.
    \label{eq:conjugate_match}
\end{equation}
The load must present a resistance equal to the coil resistance and a reactance equal in magnitude but opposite in sign to the coil reactance. Physically, the load must absorb energy from the coil (resistive part) while returning, every half cycle, exactly the reactive energy that the coil inductance would otherwise store (capacitive part). Substituting \eqref{eq:conjugate_match} into \eqref{eq:power_general} gives the maximum available power,
\begin{equation}
    P_{L,\max} \;=\; \frac{|V_c|^2}{4\,R_{Coil}},
    \label{eq:max_power}
\end{equation}
which is independent of $L_{Coil}$. Once the reactance is properly cancelled, the maximum extractable power depends only on the coil resistance, exactly as in the low frequency case of \eqref{eq:power_law}.

\subsection{Realizing the reactive compensation}

Condition \eqref{eq:reactance_cancel} shows that an inductive coil requires a load with an equal and opposite \emph{capacitive} reactance. The simplest way to obtain this is a compensation capacitor $C_m$ placed in series with the resistive load, whose reactance at $\omega_e$ is $X_{C_m} = -1/(\omega_e C_m)$. Imposing $X_{C_m} = -\omega_e L_{Coil}$ gives the required capacitance
\begin{equation}
    C_m \;=\; \frac{1}{\omega_e^2\,L_{Coil}}.
    \label{eq:matching_cap}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:matching_cap} is the condition used, for instance, to compensate the $L_{Coil}\approx210\ \text{mH}$ winding discussed at the end of Section~\ref{sec:impedance_matching}. At a given speed, a single series capacitor sized according to \eqref{eq:matching_cap} restores the resistive matching condition of \eqref{eq:resistive_match} and recovers the maximum power of \eqref{eq:max_power}.

\chapter{Theoretical Background}
\label{ch:theory}

This chapter introduces the physical and circuit-level concepts required to motivate the design choices made in the following chapters.
In this chapter, starting from Faraday's law, an electrical
model of the variable-reluctance energy harvester (VREH) is
derived. The model is then used to discuss the maximum
power transfer theorem and the role of impedance matching.

\section{Electrical Model of the Variable-Reluctance Harvester}


\subsection{Faraday's law}
\label{sec:faraday_law}

Faraday's law, which relates the electromotive force $\varepsilon(t)$ (EMF) induced in a closed loop wire to the time rate of change of the magnetic flux $\Phi(t)$ linked to the loop, is the starting point for any electromagnetic transducer.

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(t) = -\frac{\dd \Phi(t)}{\dd t}
    \label{eq:faraday}
\end{equation}

For a coil made of $N_c$ turns sharing the same flux, the total linked flux is $\Lambda(t)=N_c\Phi(t)$ and the induced EMF becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(t) = -\frac{\dd \Lambda(t)}{\dd t} = -N_c\frac{\dd \Phi(t)}{\dd t}.
    \label{eq:faraday_N}
\end{equation}

In case of a coil consisting of $N_c$ turns mechanically spaced one respect to each other leading to different linked flux, the induce voltage can be described by

\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon(t) = -\frac{d}{dt} \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{N_c} \Phi_i(t)
\label{eq:faraday_dis}
\end{equation}


where $\Phi_i$ is the flux linkage associated with a single turn of the coil \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}.

\subsection{Variable Reluctance Principle}

Unlike conventional electromagnetic transducers, in which the EMF is induced by relative motion between a permanent magnet (PM) and a pickup coil, a variable-reluctance energy harvester (VREH) keeps both the magnet and the coil stationary. The magnetic flux linked to the coil is instead modulated by the passage of a rotating, toothed ferromagnetic wheel in front of a stationary pickup unit: as a tooth or a slot alternately faces the pickup unit, the reluctance of the magnetic circuit changes periodically, and it is this reluctance variation (rather than any motion of the magnet) that drives the flux change $\Phi_i(t)$ in \eqref{eq:faraday_dis} \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}. Because the rotating part carries no magnet, no coil and no electrical connection, the resulting transducer is mechanically simple, robust, and easily scalable to shafts of different diameters \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{imm/VR_theory.pdf}
    \caption{Structure of a variable-reluctance sensor: a stationary pickup unit, composed of a permanent magnet, a soft-ferromagnetic pole-piece and a pickup coil, faces a rotating toothed wheel across an air gap.}
    \label{fig:VREH_basic_struct}
\end{figure}


The pole-piece and the toothed wheel are both made of soft ferromagnetic material, while the PM is a hard ferromagnetic material providing a magnetomotive force (MMF) $\mathcal{F}_M$ that can be taken as constant. As the wheel rotates, the pickup unit cycles between two extreme relative positions: the \emph{aligned} position, where a pole of the pickup unit faces a tooth of the wheel and the air-gap reluctance is minimum, and the \emph{unaligned} position, where the pole faces a slot and the reluctance is maximum \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018,xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}. The corresponding flux swings between a maximum $\Phi_{max}$ and a minimum $\Phi_{min}$, so that the relevant design quantity is the flux difference $\Delta\Phi = \Phi_{max}-\Phi_{min}$ between the two extremes. Fig. \ref{fig:Airgap_swing} illustrates this swing directly: the air-gap flux density rises sharply to a plateau at the aligned position and nearly collapses at the unaligned position, confirming that the flux change is concentrated within a narrow angular window around each tooth-slot transition.

\subsubsection*{From flux swing to output power}

For a wheel with $N_t$ teeth rotating at angular velocity $\omega$, the electrical frequency of the induced voltage is
\begin{equation}
    \omega_c \;=\; \omega\, N_t.
    \label{eq:freq_teeth}
\end{equation}
At low rotational speeds the coil impedance is dominated by its resistance $R_{Coil}$, so that maximum power is transferred to a purely resistive load matched to it, $R_L = R_{Coil}$; under this condition the load voltage $V_L(t)$ is related to the open-circuit coil voltage $V_c(t)$ of \eqref{eq:faraday_dis} by
\begin{equation}
    V_L(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\,V_c(t).
    \label{eq:vload}
\end{equation}
Assuming the time-varying flux to be an ideal sinusoidal wave, uniformly distributed in the $N_c$ turns of the coil, the peak-to-peak voltage developed across the matched load follows from \eqref{eq:faraday_dis} and \eqref{eq:vload} as \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}
\begin{equation}
    V_{L,pp} \;=\; -\frac{1}{2}\,N_c\,\frac{\Phi_{max}-\Phi_{min}}{\frac{1}{2}T_c} \;=\; -N_c\, f_c\, \Delta\Phi,
    \label{eq:vpp}
\end{equation}
where $\frac{1}{2}T_c$ is the time taken by the wheel to rotate from the aligned to the unaligned position, i.e.\ from a tooth to a slot centred on the pole-piece. 


Because $V_{L,pp}$ in \eqref{eq:vpp} is the peak-to-peak amplitude of an (assumed) sinusoidal load voltage, its root-mean-square value is $V_{L,rms} = V_{L,pp}/(2\sqrt{2})$, so that the RMS power dissipated in the matched load is \cite{xuSurveyVariableReluctance2018}
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;=\; \frac{V_{L,rms}^2}{R_L} \;=\; \frac{V_{L,pp}^2}{8\,R_{Coil}} \;=\; \frac{(\omega\, N_t\, N_c)^2}{8\,R_{Coil}}\,(\Delta\Phi)^2,
    \label{eq:power_law}
\end{equation}



where the last equality follows from substituting \eqref{eq:freq_teeth} into \eqref{eq:vpp}. Equation \eqref{eq:power_law} shows that the harvested power scales quadratically with the rotational speed $\omega$, the flux swing $\Delta\Phi$, the number of coil turns $N_c$ and the number of teeth $N_t$, while it is inversely proportional to the coil resistance $R_{Coil}$.

For a given application (i.e.\ fixed $\omega$ and $N_t$), the design of the pickup unit therefore splits into two largely independent optimisation problems \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    P_L &\;\propto\; \frac{N_c^2}{R_{Coil}}, \label{eq:subfunc_coil}\\
    P_L &\;\propto\; (\Delta\Phi)^2, \label{eq:subfunc_flux}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Sub-function \eqref{eq:subfunc_coil} is a winding-design problem: for a coil of resistivity $\rho_{wire}$ occupying a cross-section of height $h_{Coil}$ and width $W_{Coil}$ inside the pole-piece, it can be shown that \eqref{eq:subfunc_coil} reduces to \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;\propto\; \frac{h_{Coil}\,W_{Coil}\,k_w}{\rho_{wire}},
    \label{eq:coil_appendixA}
\end{equation}
with $k_w<1$ the winding fill factor, defined as the fraction of the winding-window cross-section $h_{Coil}W_{Coil}$ actually occupied by copper \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}. For a fixed available cross-section, output power is thus maximized simply by maximizing the copper fill of the winding window. Sub-function \eqref{eq:subfunc_flux}, in contrast, is a magnetic-circuit design problem. Once the magnet is chosen, its coercivity $H_c$ and height $h_M$ fix the MMF $\mathcal{F}_M = H_c\,h_M$, so $\Delta\Phi$ can no longer be increased by acting on the magnet alone. What remains under the designer's control is the shape of the pole-piece and toothed wheel around the air gap, thus the reluctance should be made as small as possible at the aligned position and as large as possible at the unaligned position, so that the two extremes are as far apart as possible and $\Delta\Phi$ is maximized. This magnetic-circuit optimization is detailed next.

\subsubsection*{Magnetic-circuit model of the reluctance swing}

To turn $\Delta\Phi$ into a design formula, the magnetic circuit can be treated like an electrical one, with reluctance playing the role of resistance. The reluctance of a simple flux-path element of length $l$, cross-section $A$ and relative permeability $\mu_r$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R} \;=\; \frac{l}{\mu_0\,\mu_r\,A}.
    \label{eq:reluctance_def}
\end{equation}

The pole-piece and the toothed wheel are made of soft ferromagnetic material, whose relative permeability is several orders of magnitude larger than that of air ($\mu_r\approx 1$) or of the PM ($\mu_r\approx 1.05$). Since a larger $\mu_r$ means a much smaller reluctance, the pole-piece and the wheel contribute essentially nothing to the total loop reluctance \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}. What is left, at either rotor position, is just two terms in series: the air-gap reluctance and the magnet's own reluctance,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_M \;=\; \frac{h_M}{\mu_0\,\mu_M\,A_M}.
    \label{eq:magnet_reluctance}
\end{equation}
Denoting by $\mathcal{R}_{g,a}$ and $\mathcal{R}_{g,u}$ the air-gap reluctance at the aligned and unaligned positions, Hopkinson's law gives the flux at each extreme simply as the MMF divided by the loop reluctance, $\Phi_{max}=\mathcal{F}_M/(\mathcal{R}_{g,a}+\mathcal{R}_M)$ and $\Phi_{min}=\mathcal{F}_M/(\mathcal{R}_{g,u}+\mathcal{R}_M)$. Subtracting the two gives the flux swing already introduced in \eqref{eq:subfunc_flux}, now written out explicitly:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\Phi \;=\; \mathcal{F}_M\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{g,a}+\mathcal{R}_M} - \frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{g,u}+\mathcal{R}_M}\right).
    \label{eq:flux_extremes}
\end{equation}

Once the magnet material and the nominal air-gap length are fixed by mechanical constraints, \eqref{eq:flux_extremes} shows that only two geometric parameters are left to control $\Delta\Phi$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{tooth height} $h_{Tooth}$, which affects only $\mathcal{R}_{g,u}$: a taller tooth raises the reluctance seen at the unaligned position, but this benefit saturates once the tooth is taller than about $3\ \text{mm}$, while the aligned-position reluctance $\mathcal{R}_{g,a}$ stays essentially unchanged \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a};
    \item the \textbf{magnet height} $h_M$, which pulls in two opposite directions at once: it raises the MMF $\mathcal{F}_M=H_ch_M$ (good for $\Delta\Phi$), but it also raises the magnet's own reluctance $\mathcal{R}_M$ (bad for $\Delta\Phi$). Because of this trade-off, a non-trivial optimal value of $h_M$ exists.
\end{itemize}

In a size-constrained design, what matters is not just $P_L$ but the power delivered per unit volume, i.e.\ the volumetric optimisation of the pickup unit. Modelling the harvester as a hollow cylinder of overall diameter $d_o$, shaft diameter $d_{Shaft}$ and depth $l_o$, the volumetric power density is
\begin{equation}
    p_v \;=\; \frac{P_L}{V_{VREH}}, \qquad V_{VREH} \;=\; \frac{\pi}{4}\,l_o\,(d_o-d_{Shaft})^2.
    \label{eq:power_density}
\end{equation}
Once the air-gap length and the (shared) flux-path width are fixed, the available radial space must be split between the coil and the tooth, $C = h_{Coil}+h_{Tooth}$. Growing $h_{Coil}$ always helps $P_L$, through \eqref{eq:coil_appendixA}, whereas growing $h_{Tooth}$ only helps up to the $3\ \text{mm}$ knee point described above, through \eqref{eq:flux_extremes}. Because of this asymmetry, for any given $C$ there is an optimal split between $h_{Coil}$ and $h_{Tooth}$ that maximises $p_v$ \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

The same reasoning applies to the magnet height by setting $\dv{\Delta\Phi}{h_M}=0$ in \eqref{eq:flux_extremes} yields a closed-form expression for the optimal magnet height,
\begin{equation}
    h_M^{\ast} \;=\; \mu_M\,\frac{k_2}{k_1}\,\frac{W_{g,a}}{W_{g,u}}\,\sqrt{l_{g,a}\,l_{g,u}},
    \label{eq:optimal_hm}
\end{equation}
where $l_{g,a}$, $l_{g,u}$, $W_{g,a}$, $W_{g,u}$ are the effective air-gap lengths and widths at the aligned and unaligned positions, and $k_1,k_2>1$ are correction factors that account, respectively, for flux leakage and for the MMF drop across the finite-permeability soft iron \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

\subsection{Electrical Thévenin Model of the Pickup Unit}
\label{sec:Electrical_model}
% The electrical model and its consequent derivation is important to study the system and t o of a single pick up unit 

% Il modello elettrico e la sua conseguante modellazione sono estremamente importanti e permettono di studiare il sistema dal punto di vista elettrico\cite{xu2022rotational}.



The derivation of Faraday's law in Section \ref{sec:faraday_law} gives the ideal electromotive force induced in the coil, but it does not yet describe how that voltage appears at the physical terminals of the pickup unit. A real coil is made of a finite length of copper wire, so it has a nonzero winding resistance, and it carries a current that produces its own magnetic flux, so it also has a nonzero self inductance. Both effects are in series with the ideal source, since the same wire that generates the electromotive force also carries the resistance and the flux linkage that define $R_{Coil}$ and $L_{Coil}$.

\subsubsection*{Circuit equation}

Let $e(t)$ denote the ideal electromotive force predicted by \eqref{eq:faraday_dis}, and let $i(t)$ be the current flowing out of the coil into the external circuit. Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law around the loop formed by the source, the winding resistance, the winding inductance and the external circuit gives
\begin{equation}
    v(t) \;=\; R_{Coil}\, i(t) \;+\; L_{Coil}\,\frac{\dd i(t)}{\dd t} \;+\; e(t),
    \label{eq:kvl_coil}
\end{equation}
where $v(t)$ is the voltage measured at the coil terminals, available to the power conditioning circuit. In other words, \eqref{eq:kvl_coil} models the pickup unit as an ideal voltage source $e(t)$ in series with a resistance $R_{Coil}$ and an inductance $L_{Coil}$, feeding the external circuit at the coil terminals; this series generator model is used throughout the rest of this thesis.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Circuits/Coil_model.pdf}
    \caption{Series generator model of the pickup unit: an ideal source in series with the winding resistance $R_{Coil}$ and inductance $L_{Coil}$.}
    \label{fig:coil_model}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Winding resistance}

The winding resistance follows directly from the resistivity of the wire, its total length and its cross sectional area,
\begin{equation}
    R_{Coil} \;=\; \rho_{wire}\,\frac{N_c\, l_t}{A_w},
    \label{eq:rcoil_def}
\end{equation}
where $N_c$ is the number of turns, $l_t$ is the mean length of a single turn, and $A_w$ is the cross sectional area of the wire. If the coil occupies a winding window of height $h_{Coil}$ and width $W_{Coil}$ with a copper fill factor $k_w$, the wire cross section can be written as $A_w = k_w\, h_{Coil}\, W_{Coil} / N_c$, so that \eqref{eq:rcoil_def} becomes
\begin{equation}
    R_{Coil} \;=\; \rho_{wire}\,\frac{N_c^2\, l_t}{k_w\, h_{Coil}\, W_{Coil}}.
    \label{eq:rcoil_fillfactor}
\end{equation}
Treating $l_t$ as approximately constant for a fixed pole piece geometry, \eqref{eq:rcoil_fillfactor} reproduces the proportionality $N_c^2 / R_{Coil} \propto k_w\, h_{Coil}\, W_{Coil} / \rho_{wire}$ already used in \eqref{eq:coil_appendixA} to optimise the winding cross section \cite{xuDesignModelingOptimization2019a}.

\subsubsection*{Self inductance and its dependence on rotor position}

The self inductance of a coil linked to a magnetic circuit of total reluctance $\mathcal{R}$ is, by the same reluctance used for Hopkinson's law in \eqref{eq:reluctance_def},
\begin{equation}
    L_{Coil} \;=\; \frac{N_c^2}{\mathcal{R}}.
    \label{eq:lcoil_def}
\end{equation}
The pickup unit is a variable-reluctance device, so the reluctance seen by the coil's own flux changes with the rotor angle too, in the same way as the reluctance that shapes the induced EMF. This means the self inductance is not constant: it swings between two extremes,
\begin{equation}
    L_{max} \;=\; \frac{N_c^2}{\mathcal{R}_{g,a}+\mathcal{R}_M}, \qquad
    L_{min} \;=\; \frac{N_c^2}{\mathcal{R}_{g,u}+\mathcal{R}_M},
    \label{eq:lcoil_extremes}
\end{equation}
reached at the aligned and unaligned positions, just like the flux extremes of \eqref{eq:flux_extremes}. For circuit-level analysis, such as the impedance matching study of Section \ref{sec:impedance_matching}, it is more practical to replace this varying inductance with a single, constant value $L_{Coil}$. This value can be obtained either by averaging over one electrical period, or by measuring it directly with an impedance analyser at the frequency of interest.

\subsubsection*{Phasor representation}

For a sinusoidal steady state at the electrical frequency $f_c$ of \eqref{eq:freq_teeth}, the time domain relation \eqref{eq:kvl_coil} can be written in phasor form as
\begin{equation}
    V \;=\; I\left(R_{Coil} + j\,\omega_e\,L_{Coil}\right) + E,
    \label{eq:phasor_source}
\end{equation}
where $E$, $I$ and $V$ are the phasors associated with $e(t)$, $i(t)$ and $v(t)$, and $\omega_e = 2\pi f_c$. Equation \eqref{eq:phasor_source} shows that, seen from its electrical terminals, the pickup unit behaves as an ideal source $E$ in series with the impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z_s \;=\; R_{Coil} + j\,\omega_e\,L_{Coil},
    \label{eq:source_impedance_def}
\end{equation}
which is precisely the Th\'{e}venin source impedance used in \eqref{eq:source_impedance} for the impedance matching analysis of Section \ref{sec:impedance_matching}. The series generator, resistance, and inductance model derived in this section therefore provides the electrical foundation on which the maximum power transfer condition of the next section, and the power conditioning topologies compared in Chapter \ref{ch:topologies}, are built.


\subsection{Maximum Power Transfer and Impedance Matching}
\label{sec:impedance_matching}

The previous section modelled the pickup unit as a Th\'{e}venin source, an open circuit EMF $e(t)$ in series with the coil's own impedance. At low electrical frequency this impedance is essentially the winding resistance $R_{Coil}$, and matching a resistive load to it, $R_L = R_{Coil}$, is enough to extract the maximum available power, as already used in \eqref{eq:power_law}. As the rotational speed increases, however, the coil inductance $L_{Coil}$ can no longer be neglected, and the condition for maximum power transfer must be generalized. This section derives that general condition and explains why it becomes a central design constraint for a harvester that must operate between 100 and 400\,rpm (the target range of this thesis).

\subsection{Th\'{e}venin model of the source and the load}

Let the coil be represented, at the electrical frequency of interest, by its Th\'{e}venin equivalent, an RMS open circuit phasor voltage $V_c$ in series with the source impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z_{s} \;=\; R_{Coil} \;+\; j\,\omega_e\,L_{Coil},
    \label{eq:source_impedance}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_e = 2\pi f_c$ is the electrical angular frequency and $f_c$ is the electrical frequency defined in \eqref{eq:freq_teeth}. The load presented to the coil by the power conditioning circuit is likewise represented, at the same frequency, by a generic complex impedance
\begin{equation}
    Z_{L} \;=\; R_{L} \;+\; j\,X_{L}.
    \label{eq:load_impedance}
\end{equation}
Here $R_L$ is the resistive part of the load and $X_L$ its reactive part, which can be inductive ($X_L>0$) or capacitive ($X_L<0$) depending on how the load is realised.

\subsection{Power delivered to the load}

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Circuits/Matching_condition.pdf}
    \caption{Thévenin equivalent circuit used for the impedance matching analysis: source $E$ with source impedance $Z_s$, driving a generic load impedance $Z_L$.}
    \label{fig:matching_condition}
\end{figure}

With $Z_s$ and $Z_L$ in series, the RMS current flowing in the loop is
\begin{equation}
    I \;=\; \frac{E}{Z_s + Z_L},
    \label{eq:loop_current}
\end{equation}
and the average power dissipated in the resistive part of the load is
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;=\; |I|^2 R_L \;=\; \frac{|E|^2\,R_L}{(R_{Coil}+R_L)^2 + (\omega_e L_{Coil}+X_L)^2}.
    \label{eq:power_general}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:power_general} is the generalization of \eqref{eq:power_law} to a complex load. Setting $L_{Coil}=0$ and $X_L=0$ recovers the purely resistive case already used earlier in this chapter.

\subsection{The complex conjugate matching condition}

Equation \eqref{eq:power_general} can be maximised in two independent steps, since $R_L$ and $X_L$ appear in separate terms of the denominator.

First, for any fixed $R_L$, the denominator is minimised, and $P_L$ is therefore maximised, by cancelling the reactive term. This requires
\begin{equation}
    X_L \;=\; -\,\omega_e L_{Coil}.
    \label{eq:reactance_cancel}
\end{equation}
With this choice, \eqref{eq:power_general} reduces to
\begin{equation}
    P_L \;=\; \frac{|E|^2\,R_L}{(R_{Coil}+R_L)^2}.
    \label{eq:power_resistive_stage}
\end{equation}
Second, differentiating \eqref{eq:power_resistive_stage} with respect to $R_L$ and setting the result to zero,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial P_L}{\partial R_L} \;=\; |E|^2\,\frac{R_{Coil}-R_L}{(R_{Coil}+R_L)^3} \;=\; 0
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    R_L \;=\; R_{Coil}.
    \label{eq:resistive_match}
\end{equation}
Combining \eqref{eq:reactance_cancel} and \eqref{eq:resistive_match} gives the well known maximum power transfer theorem in its general, complex form,
\begin{equation}
    Z_L \;=\; Z_s^{*} \;=\; R_{Coil} \;-\; j\,\omega_e L_{Coil}.
    \label{eq:conjugate_match}
\end{equation}
The load must present a resistance equal to the coil resistance and a reactance equal in magnitude but opposite in sign to the coil reactance. Physically, the load must absorb energy from the coil (resistive part) while returning, every half cycle, exactly the reactive energy that the coil inductance would otherwise store (capacitive part). Substituting \eqref{eq:conjugate_match} into \eqref{eq:power_general} gives the maximum available power,
\begin{equation}
    P_{L,\max} \;=\; \frac{|E|^2}{4\,R_{Coil}},
    \label{eq:max_power}
\end{equation}
which is independent of $L_{Coil}$. Once the reactance is properly cancelled, the maximum extractable power depends only on the coil resistance, exactly as in the low frequency case of \eqref{eq:power_law}.

\subsection{Realising the reactive compensation}

Condition \eqref{eq:reactance_cancel} shows that an inductive coil requires a load with an equal and opposite \emph{capacitive} reactance. The simplest way to obtain this is a compensation capacitor $C_m$ placed in series with the resistive load, whose reactance at $\omega_e$ is $X_{C_m} = -1/(\omega_e C_m)$. Imposing $X_{C_m} = -\omega_e L_{Coil}$ gives the required capacitance
\begin{equation}
    C_m \;=\; \frac{1}{\omega_e^2\,L_{Coil}}.
    \label{eq:matching_cap}
\end{equation}
Once the capacitor $C_m$ is fixed according to \eqref{eq:matching_cap}, it cancels the coil's reactance and therefore restores the resistive matching condition of \eqref{eq:resistive_match} and the maximum power of \eqref{eq:max_power} only at the single electrical frequency $\omega_e$ for which it was chosen.

\section{Multi-Phase System Model}
\label{sec:system_model}

\subsection{System Overview}

The harvester considered in this thesis is the six-phase multi-phase variable-reluctance energy harvester (MP-VREH) proposed by Wu et al. \cite{wuMultiPhaseVariableReluctance2024} and already introduced in Section \ref{sec:mpvreh_specs}, whose electrical specifications are summarised in Table \ref{tab:vreh_par}. It consists of six m-shaped pickup units, each electrically equivalent to the series generator derived in Section \ref{sec:Electrical_model}, arranged around a common toothed wheel and integrated into a single bearing hub. As the wheel rotates, each pickup unit produces an independent AC voltage at the electrical frequency $f_c=\omega N_t$ of \eqref{eq:freq_teeth}. The six pickup units are evenly spaced around the wheel, so that their induced voltages are mutually phase-shifted by $60^\circ$, one from the next.

From an electrical standpoint, the harvester can therefore be represented as six identical Th\'{e}venin sources, each with open-circuit voltage $e_k(t)$, series resistance $R_{Coil}$ and series inductance $L_{Coil}$, whose only difference is a fixed phase offset. This six-source representation is the starting point for the power conditioning circuits developed in Chapter \ref{ch:topologies}, which must process six independent AC inputs rather than a single one.

\subsection{Multi-Phase Electrical Model}

Let $\omega_e=2\pi f_c$ be the electrical angular frequency common to all six phases, and let $k=0,1,\dots,5$ index the pickup units in the order in which they are met by a tooth of the wheel. Under the ideal assumption of even angular spacing, the open-circuit EMF of phase $k$ is a sinusoid of amplitude $E_{pk}(\omega_e)$, itself a function of $\omega_e$ as in \eqref{eq:vpp}, delayed with respect to phase $0$ by $k\cdot 60^\circ$,
\begin{equation}
    e_k(t) \;=\; E_{pk}(\omega_e)\,\sin\!\left(\omega_e t - k\,\frac{\pi}{3}\right), \qquad k=0,1,\dots,5.
    \label{eq:phase_voltage}
\end{equation}
Each phase drives the power conditioning circuit through its own series impedance $Z_s=R_{Coil}+j\,\omega_e L_{Coil}$, exactly as derived for the single pickup unit in \eqref{eq:source_impedance}. Because $R_{Coil}$, $L_{Coil}$ and $E_{pk}(\omega_e)$ are the same for all six units at a given speed, the six branches differ only in the phase term of \eqref{eq:phase_voltage}, so the same circuit design can be reused for every phase, each one simply delayed in time by $60^\circ$.

\subsection{Design Requirements for the Interface Circuit}

The specifications summarised in Table \ref{tab:vreh_par} fix the operating range that the interface circuit must handle. The load voltages $V_\text{Lpk}$ reported there are measured under the impedance-matched condition of \eqref{eq:vload}, i.e.\ half of the open-circuit source amplitude $E_{pk}$ of \eqref{eq:phase_voltage} used throughout this section; over the 100-400\,rpm target speed range, this relation puts $E_{pk}$ between about $1.8\ \text{V}$ and $3.5\ \text{V}$. The rectifier must remain efficient at these low input voltages. Because the six phases are electrically identical but time-shifted, as shown in \eqref{eq:phase_voltage}, the interface circuit must either replicate the same rectification stage six times, one per phase, or combine the six inputs in a way that preserves the impedance matching condition of Section \ref{sec:impedance_matching} for each of them individually. These two requirements, low-voltage operation and per-phase impedance matching across a variable speed range, motivate the comparison of rectifying topologies carried out in Chapter \ref{ch:topologies}.
